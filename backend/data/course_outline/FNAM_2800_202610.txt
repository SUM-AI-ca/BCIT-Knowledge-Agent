### Course Description
​Humans are not separate from the land. We have been engaging in land management since time immemorial. This course explores the principles, practices and history of sustainable land management through a lens that integrates Indigenous knowledge and Western science. Students will gain a deep understanding of diverse perspectives on land stewardship, through a combination of classroom learning, field experiences, and case studies. BC is a complex and rich environment, and now more than ever we need to consider how to interact with the land in a relationship of reciprocity. Students will examine land management principles such as the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals and holistic Indigenous concepts such as the honorable harvest. Practical skills will be developed through hands-on activities like seed collection, planting, and plant propagation. Students will also learn to analyze land management information using appropriate tools and become familiar with the relevant ministries, departments, and Acts associated with resource industries in British Columbia.​
### Course Learning Outcomes/Competencies
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:
- Describe differing perspectives and worldviews relating to land management, including Indigenous perspectives such as reciprocity with the land and the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals.
- Explain holistic land management techniques, such as prescribed burning and the honorable harvest.
- Perform hands-on land management activities in the field, such as harvesting seed, planting, collecting cuttings, direct sticking, and propagating plants, demonstrating proper techniques and safety protocols.
- Analyze information related to land management in BC using appropriate tools.
- Describe important ministries, departments, and Acts associated with various BC resource industries.

### Course Goals
“The Honorable Harvest asks us to give back, in reciprocity, for what we have been given. Reciprocity helps resolve the moral tension of taking a life by giving in return something of value that sustains the ones who sustain us. One of our responsibilities as human people is to find ways to enter into reciprocity with the more-than-human world. We can do it through gratitude, through ceremony, through land stewardship, science, art, and in everyday acts of practical reverence.”
― Robin Wall Kimmerer, Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge and the Teachings of Plants

Unless otherwise agreed upon, medical appointments, interviews, etc. scheduled during class times are not considered excused absences. Students who miss more than 10% of class meetings may be given a failing grade for the course/program or the student may be removed from the course/program (Policy 5101 - Student Regulations).
Assignments (FNAM/FWR guidelines): Marks will be reduced by 10% for each 24-hour period (or fraction thereof) that an assignment is submitted beyond the due date. For instance, an assignment that would have earned 82% if submitted on time would earn 72% if submitted up to 24 hours late. If this assignment was submitted 25 hours later, it would earn 62%. The application of this penalty or alternate approaches to late submissions will be at the discretion of the course instructor.
