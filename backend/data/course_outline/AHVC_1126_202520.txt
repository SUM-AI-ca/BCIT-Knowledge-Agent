## Course Description
Students are introduced to key visual images, sculptures, and buildings in the Western cultural tradition: their features, materials, interrelationships, and social, religious, economic, and political contexts. After brief grounding in the visual culture of the ancient world and Middle Ages, the course focuses on painting, architecture / built environment, sculpture, photography and other products of visual culture in Europe and the Americas from the 1400s CE to the present. The Renaissance, Mannerism, the Baroque, Neoclassicism, Realism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Modernism and Post-Modernism are addressed. Influences on Western art from China, Japan, India, and Africa are also briefly considered. Through studying how visual culture has been defined, classified, ranked, and analyzed in Western culture – an approach established in Renaissance Europe – course participants will be equipped to contextualize the built environment, and past and current images.

## Course Learning Outcomes/Competencies
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

- Define and use standard terminology central to the study of visual culture (images, objects, buildings), including chronological and stylistic features.
- Demonstrate awareness of continuity and change in the history of visual culture, by discussing changes in subjects, themes, styles and materials in painting, sculpture, architecture, photography and other products of visual culture.
- Analyze selected works of artists and architects in specific periods in Western art.
- Identify major cultural, scientific and technological changes that significantly affected visual culture.
- Discuss the roles of artists, architects, patrons, and other leaders in developing, enforcing and critiquing artistic norms.

## Course Schedule and Assignments
Wk #: DATE Week of: TOPICS & READINGS (complete before class); Readings other than the main textbooks are available on the course site.: Assignments / Seminar topIcs
1: April 16: Course Introduction: We begin with a brief summary of art up to the Middle Ages, then take an in-depth look at art of the fourteenth century in Europe, and the beginning of the Renaissance. We will contextualize the art and architecture of the fifteenth century in Northern Europe and the Iberian peninsula in relation to the spread of humanism: Fourteenth-Century Art in Europe, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 552 – 583. Fifteenth-Century Art in Northern Europe and the Iberian Peninsula, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 584- 618.
2: April 23: Humanism in Renaissance Italy This week we consider how art of the fifteenth century in Italy reflects the values and world view of a new social order, fueled by the study of a classical revival and new scientific developments – followed by a seminar on writing about art.: Renaissance Art in Fifteenth-Century Italy, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 619 – 657. Sylvan Barnet, “Analytical Thinking,” in A Short Guide to Writing About Art, (New Jersey: Prentice Hall, 2008), pp. 47 – 99.
3: April 30: High Renaissance in Italy This week we consider art in Italy in the spirit of religious turmoil and change, as artists naviagted the murky waters of the Reformation and CounterReformation. Here we will consider how art went beyond realism to an idealism later defined by critics as Mannerism.: Sixeenth-Century Art in Italy, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 658 – 705. Giorgio Vasari, “Titian,” in The Lives of the Artists, (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1991), pp. 489-511.
4: May 7: Reformation and the Arts in Northern Europe and Spain As the Reformation created schisms throughout the Catholic world, this week we will look at how new Protestant kingdoms carried on the tradition of using the visual arts to promote a cause, to educate, and to glorify. Attention will also be given to the printing press, and the emergance of woodcuts and engravings.: Sixeenth-Century Art Northern Europe and the Iberian Peninsula, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 706 - 741
5: May 14: Renaissance and Baroque Architecture This week we devote an entire class to the architectural transformations brought about by the Renaissance and Baroque movements, where we will scrutinize the architectural tropes of buildng design, space articulation, alignment and ornament.: Sixeenth-Century Art Northern Europe and the Iberian Peninsula, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 706 - 741
6: May 21: MIDTERM EXAM: 2 Hours in Class
7: May 28: The Baroque Period Baroque art is often characterized by its exuberant and extravagant aspects. By studying the theatricality and dramatic approaches utilized by artists of the period, we will evaluate how Baroque style is aligned with an emotional rather than an intellectual response to cultural frames.: Baroque Art, , Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 742- 807.
8: June 4: Enlightened Art and Architecture This week we look at how art and architecture was shaped by the ideals of the Enlightenment, and the Era of Revolution, where we also consider the influences of Chinoiserie, and Indigenous Cultures of the Americas.: Eighteenth-Century Art in Europe and the Americas, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 984- 1063.
9: June 11: The Birth of Modern Art This week we study various artistic movements – from Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism and Impressionism, to Post-Impressionism and Japonism – to evaluate the historical landscape that led to Modern Art.: Nineteenth-Century Art in Europe and the Americas, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 984- 1063.
10: June 18: Europe and the Americas in the Early Twentieth Century From Fauvism to Cubism in art, and European Modernism to Russian Contructivism in architecture, the early twntieth century saw a systemic undermining of traditional rules of Western art. By studying the radical innovations of this period we will explore how artists of the period were invested in givng their art a deeper social meaning.: Modern Art in Europe and the Americas, 1900 – 1945. Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 1064- 1123. Michael Foucault, “The Unraveled Calligram,” in This is not a Pipe, (Los Angeles: University of California Press, 1982), pp. 19-31.
11: June 25: The Art World Since 1945 Artisitc innovation after WWII – from Abstract Expressionism and Photography to Cenceptual and Performance Art – continue to shape the Postmodern world. This week we examine art in the context of the varieties of creativity that continue to question the past as well as the future.: The International Scene Since 1945, Art History, Volume 2, Marilyn Stokstad, pp. 1124 - 1090. Final Papers Due
12: July 2: Final Exam – 2 hours; essay based
