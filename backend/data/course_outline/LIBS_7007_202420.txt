## Course Description
This course will explore the interrelationship between technology and society. The course will focus on how societal forces shape and are shaped by the meanings, development, spread, and uses of technology. Concepts, perspectives, and arguments from the social sciences and humanities will be studied and applied to analyze connections between society and technology.

## Course Learning Outcomes/Competencies
Upon successful completion, the student will be able to:

- Discuss how the creation, development, meanings, uses, and consequences of technology are shaped by various societal forces including economic, political, cultural, and psychological forces (GA 9)
- Identify, explain, and apply concepts and frameworks on the study of technology and society from various humanities and social sciences disciplines (GA 9)
- Critically engage with some of the controversies, paradoxes, and ambiguities in the relationship between technology and society (GA 9, 10, 12)
- Identify how cultural or societal ways of thinking about gender, race, and social class influence the construction, meanings, and uses of technology in society. (GA 9, 12)
- Examine technological innovations and their consequences from societal perspectives. (GA 9)
- Investigate user-technology relationships and representations. (GA 9)
- Identify issues that arise when surveying technological inventions and discoveries in world history. (GA 9, 12)
- Articulate how technology functions within diverse knowledge bases, values, and systems in different societies. (GA 9, 12)
- Discuss worldviews, power relations, and contested constructions of science and technology within and between societies. (GA 9, 10, 12)
- Examine arguments regarding the transfer and spread of technology within and across societies (GA 9, 12)
- Pursue inter-disciplinary angles on technology and society in relation to students' areas of study, work, and experience. (GA 9, 12)​

Course description This course provides a critical examination of the relationship between technology and society with the understanding that technology is not necessarily neutral, nor does it necessarily embody progress. Technology is both the driving force behind societal change as well as the output of our technological imagination. It is this dichotomy that will be examined in this course by considering technology through the lens of societal and cultural contexts, where we will try to answer the question: Do societies control technology, or does technology control societies?

Course learning outcomes / competencies: Students successfully completing this course will be able to:

- Describe specific philosophical, artistic, technological, or scientific issues presented in the course.
- Compare and evaluate representative ideas about technology from various ideological perspectives.
- Understand the implications of theory and sociological research for interpreting social events and practices.
- Apply aspects of historical ideas about technology to contemporary contexts.
- Articulate and defend a thesis about a specific topic, text or set of texts.
- Read, synthesize and evaluate theoretical arguments and published research from a critical perspective.
- Compose effective written documents analyzing a thesis, a primary text or set of texts.
- Demonstrate, to an advanced level, skills in research, citation, and document organization.

1: May 20: Course Introduction: We begin by considering Martin Heidegger’s notion of technology, and the concepts of revealing, standing reserve and enframing that define its essence.: Martin Heidegger, “The Question Concerning Technology,” in The Question Concerning Technology and Other Essays, (New York: Garland, 1977), 3 – 35.
2: May 27: Technosphere and Technocene This week we consider the possible role humans play as geological agents in service of world realms run by technology.: Helmut Trischner and Fabienne Will, “Technosphere, Technocene, and the History of Technology,” in Icon, Vol. 23 (2017), 1-17.
3: June 3: Technology and Modernity Technology as a productive force plays a particularly salient role in modern society, leading some theorists to claim that only modern societies are technological. In this class we class question the strong identification of technology with modernity.: “Modernity Theory” in Graeme Kirkpatrick, Technology and Social Power, (New York: Palgrave MacMillan, 2008), 38-57.
4: June 10: Technology as Reproduction Since the development of the camera in the 19th century, the progress of reproduction of the visual world has made leaps and bounds. In this class we look at the technology of reproduction – from the art of film to the “selfie” – and consider the serious consequences for it poses for society.: Walter Benjamin, “The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction,” in Illuminations, teans. Harry Zohn, ed. by Hannah Arendt, (New York: Schocken Books, 1968), 217-253.
5: June 17: Technology and Humour Drawing on Henri Bergson’s On Laughter, and Charlie Chaplin’s film Modern Times, in this class we continue our exploration with pivitol insights humour provides for understanding society’s relationship with technology.: Steffen Steinert, “Technology is a Laughing Matter: Bergson, the Comic and Technology,” in AI & Society, September 2015. https://vimeo.com/680913368
6: June 24: Technology and Ethics This week we revisit Heidegger’s philosophy of technology, where we focus on the epitemological and ontological questions central to Heidegger’s thought on modern technology and how it can be used to evaluate the relationship between society and technology.: Anabel Quan-Hasse, “Ethical Dimensions of Technology,” in, Technology and Society: Social Networks, Power and Inequality, (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2013), 215-229.
7: July 1: Technology and the Politics of Memory Themistocles (524 – 459 BCE) once commented: “I would rather a technique of forgetting, for I remember what I would rather not remember and cannot forget what I would rather forget.” In this class we explore mnemotechnologies, particularly cell phones.: James Poulos, “The Technology of Memory,” The New Atlantis , Spring 2008, No. 20 (Spring 2008), pp. 111-119. Sherry Turkle, “Always on/Always on You: The Tethered Self,” in Handbook of Mobile Communication Studies, James E. Katz (ed.). (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2008), 121-137.
8: July 8: Language as Technology Language is a natural collective technology that evolved to facilitate efficient communication in populations whose social structures had become increasingly more complex. It consists of alphabets or logographies, that have often been compared to architecture topography. In this class we consider the technology of language and its role in society.: Richard Sproat, “Writing as a Language Technology” in Language, Technology and Society, (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2010), 9-29.
9: July 15: The Politics of Alternative Technologies This week we look at technology from an engineer’s perspective, highlighting the importance of collaboration where we consider the following questions: What is the ideology of technology? What role do maintenance and humanitarian concerns play in technological creativity?: Matthew Wisnioski, “Three Bridges to Creative Renewal” in Engineers for Change: Competing Visions of Technology in 1960s America, (Cambridge: MIT Press, 2012), 41-65.
10: July 24: The Myth of AI? This week we consider technology through the lens of Erik J. Larson’s pivitol work The Myth of Artificial Intelligence, where we will question the inherent differences between computers and humans.: Erik J. Larson, “The Intelligence Error,” in The Myth of Artificial Intelligence: Why Computers Can’t Think the Way We Do, (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 2021), 9-18.
11: July 29: CRISPR/Cas9 This week we will scrutinize this powerful, new gene-editing technology and its many applications in biomedical research and genetic engineering: CRISPR video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jAhjPd4uNFY&t=19s
11: August 5: The Technology of Surveillance From CCTV to IP addresses, modern technologies of surveillance are ubiquitous. In this class we consider the development of surveillance technologies from an historical perspective, and their implications for the future.: “The Surveillance Society,” in Anabel Quan-Hasse, Technology and Society: Social Networks, Power and Inequality, (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2013), 191-213.
13: August 8: Final Exam – 2 hours; essay based
