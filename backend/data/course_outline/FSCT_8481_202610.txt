### Course Description
This course will introduce you to the various structured analytical techniques that are commonly used throughout the Crime Analysis Cycle as it is applied in Canadian violent crime investigations. You will learn the best practices in analytical software, techniques, and methods used by analysts in major case management for violent crimes, such as homicide, serious assaults, sex offences, extortion, kidnapping, and missing persons. You will also learn how to collect and evaluate publicly available information when completing analytical tasks for crimes of violence.
### Course Learning Outcomes/Competencies
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:
- Apply critical-thinking skills to assess the relevance, reliability, and validity of data collected during violent crime investigations.
- Use GIS software to prepare crime maps for violent crimes analysis, using temporal and spatial data.
- Analyze a variety of sources to develop offender profiles and timelines.
- Identify crime patterns and similar fact analysis in relation to a violent crime, such as analyzing witness statements and open source information.
- Prepare a threat assessment related to an individual’s propensity for violence.
- Complete an intermediate-level communications analysis, which includes phone records and cell tower information.
- Create an intermediate-level link association, timeline, or event flow, and commodity flow charts for violent crime investigation.
- Design and prepare an intermediate tactical crime analysis bulletin related to violent crime.
- Prepare a report and then give a verbal presentation of your analysis that could be used as court testimony.

### Course Goals
Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:
• Discuss the different types of investigative techniques associated with major crime investigations.
• Describe the role of an analyst working in a major case management (MCM) setting.
• Conduct a range of analytic techniques including suspect identification, tracker analysis, communication analysis, cell dump analysis, association analysis, and timeline.
• Recommend investigation avenues with sound analytical findings.
• Prepare effective analytical products and present findings in a logical and organized manner.
• Apply critical thinking to solve challenges associated with analyzing imperfect data.
• Describe the requirements of disclosure provisions and the stages of a court process.
• Discuss the role of the analyst in post-case evaluations and de-briefing.

DISCUSSIONS (9%) Students will be asked to write a short self-reflection post on a selected topic and respond to two classmates’ comments. Grading is based on the student’s knowledge and understanding of content and ability to make connections with other learnings. ASSIGNMENTS (45%) Assignments are to be uploaded to the Learning Hub and information will be visible within the weekly folder that the assignment is due. The only acceptable formats are Adobe PDF or other specified formats. Assignments will be graded on grammar, spelling, theoretical comprehension, practical application, critical thinking, organization and overall presentation. A sound command of the English language at is expected using font 12, Times New Roman or Arial, and 1.5 spacing.
GROUP PROJECT (16%) Students will be assigned into one of four real case studies. Selected information pertaining to each case will be given to each group after each class. The task is to compile and analyze the investigative materials provided as a group and present a 5–10-minute presentation in classes 2 through 5.
FINAL EXAM (25%) Students will be given 75 minutes to complete 6 scenario-based questions. The purpose of each question is to assess the student’s ability to examine different types of data sets in different situations, to correctly determine the appropriate analytical technique that should be applied, to logically articulate the meaning of possible results, and/or to accurately interpret an analytical product.
PARTICIPATION (5%) Learning is enhanced through student participation and class exercises. These interactions will be an important aspect of the curriculum
