## Course Description
Develops simple, yet powerful methods for understanding and evaluating a wide variety of scientific and pseudo scientific material. Introduces some of the great thinkers and theories of the past, both winners and losers. Reflects on what makes scientific reasoning so effective, and uses these reflections to evaluate some contemporary criticisms of the place of science in society.

## Course Learning Outcomes/Competencies
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

- Understand the role of the philosophy of science in critically evaluating the methods, goals, and process of science. [12]
- Demonstrate familiarity with prominent influences in the philosophy of science. [12]
- Identify and understand the kinds of arguments used by philosophers of science. [12]
- Demonstrate facility with the methods and terminology used by thinkers in the field. [7,12]
- Critically analyze philosophical arguments and relevant texts at an appropriate level. [7, 12]
- Source material relevant to forming, defending, or critiquing positions on issues in the philosophy of science. [12]
- Evaluate sources in terms of their authority, and the cogency and consistency of their argumentation. [12]
- Develop critical writing skills. [7]

## Course Goals
This course introduces students to the history and philosophy of science; it also seeks to improve their ability to understand and evaluate popular and semi-technical science reporting.

Science raises many philosophical questions. What is the structure of scientific reasoning? Can science ever provide sufficient reason to believe any scientific theory? Is scientific knowledge restricted to things we can directly observe? Is contemporary science merely a culturally biased “story” of the world? Learning about the philosophy of science teaches students to intelligently evaluate criticisms and defences of the place of science in contemporary culture.

Science also has a fascinating history, in which currently accepted theories won the support of the scientific community over theories that, taken in the context of their own times, would have presented worthy opposition. A study of historical scientific episodes not only enriches students’ understanding of intellectual history, it also elucidates why our scientific predecessors believed in theories that now seem obviously flawed. Learning about the history of science helps students to become aware of the assumptions and biases of currently accepted theories and to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of contemporary research directions. Ultimately, students are encouraged to see science not merely as facts, definitions and equations to memorize, but a tentative, evolving engagement with nature.

## Course topics
Hours: Topics
2: Introduction What is philosophy? What is philosophy of science? What is science? What are the origins of science? What is the aim of science?
3: Inductivism, Rationalism and Empiricism Bacon’s inductivism, scientific method, nature of scientific explanation Cartesian rationalism Mathematization of science Humean empiricism, induction, causation Kant and the structure of experience
2: Historical Case Study: Theory of Heat Phlogiston, caloric, average kinetic energy More than one hypothesis can explain a given body of experimental findings Entities postulated by a theory guide the choice of experiments and their interpretation
3: Logical Positivism Evolution of Kantian schools of thought towards logical positivism Early Wittgenstein Verificationism Paradox of confirmation
2: Historical Case Study: Freud and Psychoanalysis Project for a scientific psychology Psychotherapy Behaviorism
3: Karl Popper Problem of demarcation, science and pseudoscience Falsifiability Explanatory power Induction Criteria for simplicity, rules of inference
3: Historical Case Study: Einstein and Relativism Machian relativism vs. Newton’s absolute space The luminiferous æther and the Michelson-Morley experiment Length contraction, time dilation and special relativity General relativity, Mach’s principle, frame dragging
6: Thomas Kuhn Phases in the structure of scientific revolutions Pre-paradigm “Normal science,” puzzle-solving Anomaly, crisis, paradigm shift, incommensurability Paradigm dependence Invisibility of revolutions
3: Historical Case Study: The Copernican Revolution Movement in the heavens Ptolemaic cosmogony, retrograde motion Copernican cosmogony, comparison of models Kepler, astronomy vs. physics
6: Contemporary Case Study: Foundations of Quantum Theory Double slit experiment, photoelectric effect, wave-particle duality Uncertainty principle, Copenhagen interpretation, meaning of probability Scientific realism vs. instrumentalism Schrodinger’s cat, EPR paradox, Bell’s inequality Alternative interpretations: de Broglie-Bohm-Hiley, many worlds, consistent histories, objective collapse, relational interpretation
3: Contemporary Case Study: Dark Matter Dark matter hypothesis, galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, cosmic microwave background MACHOs versus WIMPs, experimental searches Alternative hypotheses, MOND, external field effect and Mach's principle Incommensurabilty, Bullet Cluster
3: Exams and Review
